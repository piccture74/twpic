## Case Report：Fascial Manipulation
I will present a case report of fascial manipulation.
The patient is a 25-year-old female athletic trainer. She is a runner and also goes to the gym, although running is her main activity.
## Patient History
The patient complained of pain in the left shoulder that had been present since around 2026-10-26. The pain intensity ranged from 5 to 8, and there was no trauma. External rotation made the pain worse.
She also had pain on the left lateral side of the neck since 2026-11-22, also without trauma. The pain intensity ranged from 5 to 6, and left rotation made it worse.
In addition, she had a right wrist problem. The pain ranged from 0 to 3 and was slightly aggravated during flexion. She also reported some numbness in the feet, especially in the right foot.
There were several important points in her previous history. She had a previous shoulder problem on the right side on 2025-11-26, and it was exactly like what she was experiencing on the left side at the time of this case. She also had gallbladder removal and multiple ankle sprains, particularly around 2016-11-26.
This was the history of the patient.
## Movement Evaluation
I started with movement evaluation. Since the patient was in pain, I wanted to see clearly how movement made the symptoms worse. I evaluated the patient in the sagittal, frontal, and horizontal planes, focusing on the segments that were more altered. In this case, those segments were the left humerus and the neck.
In the movement assessment, there was an important limitation in elevation, an important limitation in abduction, and important limitations in both external and internal rotation. I wrote down this information as follows：humeral ante-left with two asterisks, lateral left with two asterisks, one intra-left, and one extra-left.
The neck also presented some limitation, especially in right lateral bending and a little bit in external rotation.
## Palpation and Fascial Planes
After movement evaluation, I moved on to palpation.
When I say palpation, I mean that I have to palpate the deep fascia in order to evaluate which plane is the worst. It can be the frontal plane. It can also be the horizontal plane, where the collagen fibers move in an oblique direction. Or it can be the sagittal plane, where the collagen fibers move in a longitudinal direction.
Using the iliac bone as an example, the deep fascia clearly has collagen fibers in multiple directions：transverse, oblique, and longitudinal collagen fibers. I had to determine which of these structures was generating more problems for the patient.
## Initial Hypothesis
The key point at this stage was to create a hypothesis and decide which segments I wanted to palpate in the three planes.
My hypothesis was this：there was no trauma at all. Instead, there was a lot of overuse related to her work as an athletic trainer, her running, and her gym activity.
She had a previous right shoulder problem on 2025-11-26 without trauma, and it was very similar to what she was experiencing on the left side in this case. In my opinion, there was a lot of tension in the trunk. This tension probably generated symptoms on the right side once, and now probably generated symptoms on the left side.
Therefore, my idea was to palpate the thorax as the original problem, and also the scapula, which is near the humerus where she felt pain. The humerus could be quite intense to touch because the condition was quite acute on 2026-11-26. In my opinion, the symptom came from there.
However, if I chose the thorax and humerus, that would still be fine. It would not be a problem. Choosing the thorax and scapula, or the thorax and humerus, would both be acceptable.
## Transversal Palpation
I then moved on with transversal palpation. I palpated transversal, anterolateral, and interdextra. However, for the thorax, I did not palpate medial, because medial does not work for motion. It is a line of perception alignment, so in this case I could avoid palpating there.
The result was that the sagittal and horizontal planes were quite similar. Both were altered. In one area, there were six stars, around three for each, and two asterisks for the boxes. In another area, there were two boxes with two, and then a three with one. The findings were very similar, so I was not able to decide which plane was the worst.
## Adding Another Segment
Because I could not decide from the initial findings, I wanted to palpate another segment.
I decided to go down to the arm and double-check whether there was any overuse there. The reason was that she lifts weights when she is in the gym, and this can generate a lot of overuse in the arm. I chose the cubitus, although the carpus could also have been chosen.
I needed some data in the extremity of the upper limb to understand whether there was any origin or differentiation there.
The cubitus helped a lot, because it added another three asterisks in the horizontal plane. This meant that the horizontal plane was definitely the worst at that moment. The horizontal plane was the plane giving the patient the most problems, and if I treated the horizontal plane, it would be the one that would give the best result to the patient.
Therefore, the horizontal plane was the plane I was going to treat on 2026-11-26. I would not touch the frontal or sagittal plane.
## Longitudinal Palpation in the Horizontal Plane
Next, I proceeded with longitudinal palpation. By this, I mean that although I had already palpated three segments, I wanted to know a little more about intra-extra hepatic and intra-extra articular findings, meaning I wanted more information about the horizontal plane.
Basically, I continued the palpation by trying to palpate segmented data that I had not palpated before. Again, I palpated intra-extra humerus and intra-extra carpus.
This should help because I would expect to find other densifications in these segments. This would confirm that the plane was very altered and would generate many symptoms.
In addition, it would help me understand where the core of the problem was and where the periphery of the problem was. Then I could organize the proximal-distal detection, agonist-antagonist, and the principles that were seen in strategy.
The longitudinal palpation showed two stars and two boxes in one area. There was also one with three stars in the carpus, so the intra-carpal point was very altered. This showed that there was radiation going a little bit down and even up.
The intra-carpal point was therefore a key segment that had to be treated. This palpation pushed me to think that there was also a peripheral overuse component. There was overuse in the trunk and overuse in the wrist.
## Treatment Strategy
At this point, I wanted to start the treatment. In treatment, I had to start from the worst point, the point that was generating the most problems. Therefore, I wanted to start from the intra-carpal point.
The intra-carpal point was the first point, and it was far away from the area of the symptom. Interestingly, it was not so altered in the area of the symptom itself, but the intra-carpal point was extremely altered and generated a lot of radiation. Therefore, it was a good point to start with.
The intra-carpal point is located at the musculotendinous junction of the flexors, in the middle of the forearm. It can be treated easily with the elbow flat, in order to spread the tension. This is important because the median nerve is right below this area. I would therefore expect numbness, pins and needles. The patient would probably start to complain on and off, because this densification typically generates this kind of symptom.
I treated the point until the tissue became softer and smoother.
## Immediate Response After the First Point
After treating the intra-carpal point, the patient immediately felt an 80％ to 90％ improvement. The pain at rest dropped almost to zero, and the movements were already much better.
However, the result was not 100％. There was still some pain during some movements.
## Choosing the Second Point
Next, I could go proximally. I palpated intra-tarsal and intra-scapular points, but these almost disappeared.
At that moment, the more important thing was to balance. To balance the intra-sequence, I needed an extra point. In this case, I had the extra thorax point.
Therefore, extra thorax was the next point to treat. When I palpated that point, it was still altered, which meant that it needed to be treated. In contrast, the other points had dropped just by treating intra-carpal.
This changed my hypothesis a little more. There was probably a double overuse at the distal part of the upper limb. Therefore, the wrist and forearm region was probably more important than the other area. I also believed that the other forearm, the right one, would be the same.
The extra thorax point is located in the superior scapulothoracic area. I wanted to work with the elbow, the medial border of the scapula, or the shoulder blade. I worked at the end of the spine of the shoulder blade and went underneath to try to reach the superior scapulothoracic area.
I continued working until the tissue became softer and the patient no longer complained of sharp pain, which could probably refer to the shoulder.
## Comparison Before and After Treatment
Before the treatment, there was a major limitation in abduction and elevation. There was also a lot of limitation in internal and external rotation. The patient clearly had major limitations in those movements.
The treatment consisted of two points：intra-carpal and extra thorax.
After the treatment, the patient had full range of motion. Basically, she no longer complained of pain with movement after only two points.
Even more importantly, the strength came back. I checked the strength by asking the patient to hold on and not let me push the arm down. The patient was able to resist my force. She did not complain of pain, weakness, or any difference between one arm and the other.
## Conclusion
With only a couple of points and a few minutes, we restored a severe acute shoulder case that had been present since around 2026-10-26, or even longer. She had already received different treatments, but she had not responded to them.
If I follow a very methodical process, as in fascial manipulation, and go through collection, history, movement, hypothesis, palpation, and treatment, I can achieve an amazing result immediately. I can clearly see that the results are there.
The result should last longer because I know the situation, I saw what was wrong, and I saw how to improve it.
